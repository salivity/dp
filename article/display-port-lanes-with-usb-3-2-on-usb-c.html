DisplayPort Lanes with USB 3.2 on USB-C

When running DisplayPort video and USB 3.2 data concurrently over a single USB Type-C cable, DisplayPort utilizes exactly two high-speed lanes. This article explains how the USB Type-C connector allocates its physical high-speed data paths to balance video transmission with SuperSpeed data transfers, what this means for display resolution, and how this mode compares to dedicated video-only configurations.

Physical Lane Allocation on USB Type-C

A standard USB Type-C connector contains four high-speed differential pairs (lanes) capable of carrying high-bandwidth signals, designated as TX1/RX1 and TX2/RX2. In standard operation, these lanes are dynamically configured through DisplayPort Alternate Mode (DP Alt Mode) negotiation between the host and the peripheral.

When a dock, monitor, or adapter requires simultaneous USB 3.2 Gen 1 (5 Gbps) or USB 3.2 Gen 2 (10 Gbps) data connectivity alongside video output, the connector splits these four high-speed lanes evenly:

  • 2 High-Speed Lanes for DisplayPort: Carries the video and audio stream.
  • 2 High-Speed Lanes for USB 3.2: Allocates one transmit pair (TX) and one receive pair (RX) for full-duplex SuperSpeed data.

Impact on Display Bandwidth and Resolution

Using two lanes cuts the raw video bandwidth in half compared to a native four-lane DisplayPort connection. However, modern DisplayPort standards still achieve high resolutions over two lanes depending on the link rate:

  • DisplayPort 1.2 (HBR2): Two lanes deliver a maximum data rate of 8.64 Gbps, which supports uncompressed 1080p at 144Hz or 4K at 30Hz.
  • DisplayPort 1.4 (HBR3): Two lanes deliver up to 12.96 Gbps. Using Display Stream Compression (DSC), this configuration easily drives 4K displays at 60Hz with HDR.
  • DisplayPort 2.1 (UHBR10/13.5): Two lanes can provide upwards of 20 to 26 Gbps, enabling uncompressed 4K at 60Hz and higher refresh rates even while sharing the cable with USB 3.2.

Comparison with 4-Lane DisplayPort Mode

If an external display requires the full bandwidth of four DisplayPort lanes, the USB-C interface reallocates all four high-speed pairs entirely to video. In this scenario, simultaneous USB 3.2 data is disabled, and any attached peripherals (such as keyboard and mouse ports on a monitor) fall back to USB 2.0 speeds. This fallback works because USB 2.0 operates over dedicated, separate D+ and D- pins within the USB Type-C connector, completely independent of the four high-speed lanes.
