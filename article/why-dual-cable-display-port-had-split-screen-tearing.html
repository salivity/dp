Why Dual-Cable DisplayPort Had Split-Screen Tearing

Early 4K monitors often relied on dual-cable DisplayPort connections or Multi-Stream Transport (MST) tiling to achieve a 60Hz refresh rate before single-cable hardware matured. However, users frequently encountered noticeable screen tearing directly down the center of the panel, where the left and right halves appeared out of alignment during motion. This article explains the technical reasons behind this split-screen tearing, focusing on how independent display pipelines, clock drift, and the absence of hardware frame synchronization affected early dual-cable setups.

The Need for Tiled Displays

When 4K monitors first entered the consumer market, internal display timing controllers (TCONs) and standard video interfaces lacked the bandwidth to process a single 3840x2160 stream at 60Hz. To bypass these hardware limits, panel manufacturers designed displays as two distinct vertical halves (each measuring 1920x2160). The monitor was effectively two physical screens housed inside a single bezel, requiring either two separate DisplayPort cables or a single cable using Multi-Stream Transport to feed two distinct video signals simultaneously.

Independent Display Pipelines

Because the monitor reported itself as two separate panels, the host graphics card had to treat the display as a multi-monitor array. The GPU relied on display stitching drivers (such as AMD Eyefinity or NVIDIA Surround) to present both halves as a single logical desktop to the operating system.

Internally, the GPU pushed two distinct video streams through two separate display output engines. Each stream operated with its own:

  • Pixel clock: The frequency at which individual pixels are transmitted.
  • Scanout timing: The path tracing pixels line-by-line from top to bottom.
  • Vertical Blanking Interval (V-Blank): The brief pause signaling the end of a frame.

Lack of Hardware Genlock and Phase Drift

Consumer graphics cards lacked the dedicated hardware frame-locking (genlock) found in professional workstation hardware. Without hardware-level synchronization, the two DisplayPort transmitters could not stay permanently locked in phase.

Microscopic manufacturing differences in clock oscillators and slight variations in transmission timing caused clock jitter. Over time, the refresh cycles of the two halves drifted apart. Even a microsecond difference in phase meant that the left side of the screen and the right side of the screen were never scanning lines at the exact same physical position at the exact same time.

Desynchronized Page Flipping

The most visible issue occurred during frame rendering in games and video playback. When an application completed rendering a frame, the GPU executed a "page flip" to switch the front and back display buffers.

Because the two screen halves ran on independent display engines, the page flip did not occur simultaneously across both outputs. One cable’s scanout engine might catch the new frame before its V-Blank interval closed, while the other cable missed the window and had to wait for the next refresh cycle. Consequently, for a fraction of a second, the left half of the display showed frame N+1 while the right half remained on frame N. This created a prominent vertical tear right along the center seam of the panel.

How the Issue Was Resolved

Split-screen tearing eventually disappeared as display technology progressed on two fronts:

  1. Higher-Bandwidth Interfaces: The release of DisplayPort 1.3 and 1.4 provided sufficient bandwidth to carry 4K signals at high refresh rates over a single lane without splitting the signal.
  2. Unified TCONs and Single-Stream Transport (SST): Display manufacturers developed scalable timing controllers capable of processing the entire panel resolution natively as a single image buffer.

Once GPUs could transmit the entire frame over a single stream to a single scaler, the need for dual pipelines and tiled rendering ended, permanently eliminating center-line seam tearing.
