DisplayPort Redrivers vs Retimers Explained

Maintaining signal integrity over extended trace lengths or long cables is a major challenge in modern DisplayPort implementations. As data rates climb with DisplayPort standards like HBR3 and UHBR, high-frequency signals attenuate quickly. Signal boosters and redrivers address this by applying analog amplification and equalization to the existing signal, which also passes along any underlying timing jitter and noise. In contrast, full retimer chips extract the data, discard the jitter using Clock and Data Recovery (CDR), and regenerate a completely fresh, re-clocked signal from scratch, offering a far more robust but costlier solution for demanding high-bandwidth environments.

What Are Signal Boosters and Redrivers?

Signal boosters and redrivers are analog-only signal conditioning devices designed to counteract channel loss caused by resistance, dielectric attenuation, and reflections on printed circuit boards (PCBs) or through cables.

  • How they work: A redriver uses Continuous Time Linear Equalization (CTLE) at the input to compensate for high-frequency losses and applies pre-emphasis and gain at the output stage to amplify the signal amplitude.
  • Jitter handling: Redrivers do not reconstruct the signal. While they open a closed "eye" diagram by restoring amplitude, they amplify both the desired data and the accumulated random and deterministic jitter.
  • Latency and power: Because they operate purely in the analog domain without decoding the stream, redrivers introduce negligible latency (sub-nanosecond) and consume very little power.
  • Use case: They are ideal for short-to-medium PCB trace extensions, active cables for older standards (such as DisplayPort 1.2 or 1.4 at reduced lengths), and budget-conscious hardware designs.

What Are Full Retimer Chips?

A retimer is a mixed-signal, protocol-aware physical layer (PHY) component. Instead of simply amplifying an incoming wave, a retimer terminates the signal and retransmits it.

  • How they work: The retimer receives the degraded analog signal, equalizes it, and uses a Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) circuit to sample the bits and recover the raw data. It then uses an internal local reference clock to generate an entirely new physical signal with sharp edges and ideal voltage levels.
  • Jitter handling: A retimer completely resets the channel's jitter budget. Because the CDR strips away phase noise and timing jitter from the previous segment, the transmitted signal effectively starts with a fresh signal-to-noise ratio.
  • Latency and power: Retiming requires processing the bitstream, which introduces measurable latency (typically tens of nanoseconds) and consumes significantly more power than an analog redriver. They also generate more heat, often requiring dedicated thermal considerations.
  • Use case: Retimers are mandatory for long transmission paths, multi-hop topologies, USB-C alternate mode docks, and the ultra-high speeds of DisplayPort 2.0/2.1 (UHBR 10, 13.5, and 20 Gbps per lane).

Key Differences at a Glance

Feature Signal Booster / Redriver Full Retimer Chip
Signal Domain Purely analog Mixed-signal (Analog-Digital-Analog)
Clock Handling None (passes clock embedded in data) Full Clock and Data Recovery (CDR)
Jitter Performance Amplifies accumulated jitter Resets the jitter budget entirely
Latency Extremely low (< 1 ns) Low, but higher than redrivers (~10–50 ns)
Cost & Complexity Low cost, simple integration High cost, complex routing and firmware
Power Consumption Minimal Moderate to high
Ideal Standards DP 1.2 / DP 1.4 (short reaches) DP 1.4 (long reaches) / DP 2.1 (UHBR)

Choosing Between the Two

The choice between a redriver and a retimer comes down to the frequency of the DisplayPort signal and the total loss of the channel:

  • Choose a redriver when you need a simple, low-cost fix for a few inches of extra PCB trace between a GPU and a display port, or in short-range active cables where overall jitter remains well within compliance limits.
  • Choose a retimer when designing high-performance docking stations, active extension cables, or systems using DisplayPort 2.1 UHBR transmission rates. At bandwidths approaching 20 Gbps per lane, analog equalization alone cannot resolve signals through standard channels without violating strict jitter specifications.
