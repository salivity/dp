Why Long DisplayPort HPD Drops Cause Disconnections

When using a DisplayPort connection, the Hot Plug Detect (HPD) line acts as the primary communication bridge for physical presence detection and hardware interrupts between a monitor and a computer. A brief voltage drop on the HPD line signals an interrupt request, prompting the graphics card to read status registers, whereas an excessively long drop exceeds the standard threshold defined by VESA specifications. Once this low-signal threshold is breached, the graphics driver can no longer distinguish between a temporary glitch and an intentional cable removal, forcing the system to tear down the active display pipeline and register a complete device disconnection.

The Role of Hot Plug Detect in DisplayPort

The DisplayPort interface uses dedicated physical pins to establish and maintain a video link. While high-speed lanes carry video data and the AUX channel handles metadata like Extended Display Identification Data (EDID), the HPD line (Pin 18) operates as a simple DC-voltage signaling line (nominally 3.3V) driven by the sink (monitor) and monitored by the source (computer/GPU).

The monitor keeps the HPD line held high to indicate it is powered, connected, and ready to receive data. The computer monitors this state to determine when to allocate hardware resources, configure resolutions, and stream video payloads.

The Timing Difference: IRQ Pulse vs. Unplug Event

The DisplayPort standard defines two distinct behaviors for when the HPD signal drops to a low state (0V):

  1. HPD Interrupt (HPD_IRQ): A short low pulse lasting between 0.5 milliseconds and 1.0 millisecond. This short pulse tells the GPU that the display remains connected, but something has changed—such as an HDCP handshake request, an audio status update, or an automated request for link retraining.
  2. Disconnection Event (Plug/Unplug): Any continuous low state lasting longer than 2.0 milliseconds. When the line stays low beyond this period, the source hardware treats it not as an interrupt, but as a loss of physical connection.

Why an Excessively Long Drop Triggers a Full Disconnect

When electrical interference, monitor firmware lag, or bad cabling causes the HPD line to stay low well past the 2.0-millisecond mark, the source initiates a standard disconnect sequence:

  • Hardware Link Teardown: The GPU immediately disables its main link transmitters to conserve power and protect the high-speed signaling circuitry.
  • Flushing of Cached Metadata: The graphics driver purges the stored DisplayPort Configuration Data (DPCD) and EDID information for that port, assuming the monitor has been physically unplugged.
  • Operating System Notification: The graphics driver reports the disconnect to the operating system’s window manager. This causes the operating system to reorganize open windows, disable the corresponding desktop workspace, and shift active applications to a secondary monitor or default virtual resolution.

Even if the HPD line returns to high immediately after this prolonged drop, the connection cannot simply resume where it left off. The GPU must treat the rising edge as a completely new device arrival, requiring a full re-read of the EDID via the AUX channel, a fresh link training sequence to negotiate lane count and voltage swing, and a display reconfiguration at the OS level.

Common Causes of Prolonged HPD Drops

Excessive drops on the HPD line rarely stem from intentional disconnection. They are usually caused by:

  • Monitor Sleep and Power States: Poorly configured monitor firmware may drop the HPD line for several seconds when transitioning to deep sleep or switching inputs, tricking the computer into thinking the display was removed.
  • High Line Capacitance and Long Cables: Excessively long or out-of-spec passive cables can introduce parasitic capacitance, which slows down the rise time of the HPD line and keeps the voltage below the logic-high threshold for too long.
  • KVM Switches and Adapters: Inexpensive or non-emulating KVM switches drop the HPD pin completely when switching between systems, resulting in desktop rearrangement and resolution resets on the disconnected machine.
