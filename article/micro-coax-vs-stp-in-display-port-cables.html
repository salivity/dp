Micro-Coax vs STP in DisplayPort Cables

Modern DisplayPort standards demand unprecedented bandwidth, making internal cable architecture a vital factor in video performance. This article compares micro-coaxial (micro-coax) and shielded twisted pair (STP) wire constructions used in DisplayPort cables, breaking down their differences in signal integrity, electromagnetic interference (EMI) protection, mechanical flexibility, and production costs, especially as standards advance toward DisplayPort 2.1.

Structural Differences

Shielded twisted pair (STP) cabling consists of two insulated copper conductors twisted together along their longitudinal axis, wrapped in a metallic foil shield and sometimes combined with an overall braided outer shield. In a standard DisplayPort cable, four main STP lanes handle the high-speed data transmission.

Micro-coaxial construction replaces these twisted pairs with individual coaxial lines. Each signal wire features a single center conductor surrounded by a dielectric insulator, a continuous concentric metallic shield, and an outer jacket. Rather than grouping two signal paths into a single twisted unit, micro-coax transmits signals across isolated, miniature coaxial lines.

Signal Integrity and High-Frequency Performance

As DisplayPort standards have advanced from DP 1.4 (HBR3 at 32.4 Gbps) to DP 2.1 (UHBR20 at up to 80 Gbps), signal attenuation and intra-pair skew have become major engineering bottlenecks.

  • Intra-Pair Skew: In STP cables, slight variations in the twist rate, physical tension, or dielectric uniformity cause time-domain delay differences between the two conductors in a differential pair. At ultra-high bit rates, this skew degrades the signal eye diagram, limiting cable length. Micro-coaxial cables minimize intra-pair skew because each conductor is independently shielded and manufactured to tight concentric tolerances.
  • Impedance Control: Micro-coax maintains a more consistent characteristic impedance across high frequencies due to its uniform radial geometry. STP cables are prone to localized impedance discontinuities wherever the twist geometry shifts or compresses when bent.

EMI and Crosstalk Suppression

Cross-talk occurs when high-frequency energy leaks from one transmission lane to another.

  • Micro-Coax: Each individual conductor features 360-degree shielding. This architecture isolates each lane, preventing near-end crosstalk (NEXT) and far-end crosstalk (FEXT) almost entirely. Radiated EMI is also minimal, which is essential for preventing interference with nearby Wi-Fi and Bluetooth antennas.
  • STP: Shielding encloses the entire pair rather than each individual conductor. While effective at canceling common-mode noise via the differential twists, high-frequency leakage can still occur between adjacent pairs if the foil wrapping shifts or degrades.

Cable Diameter, Flexibility, and Durability

Micro-coaxial cables are significantly thinner and more flexible than STP equivalents:

  • Outer Diameter: A standard 28–30 AWG STP DisplayPort cable typically measures 6.5 mm to 7.5 mm in outer diameter. A comparable micro-coax cable can achieve an outer diameter under 4.5 mm.
  • Bend Radius: Micro-coax allows for tighter bend radiuses without pinching or distorting the dielectric. This makes it the preferred solution for tethered VR headsets, thin laptop docking cables, and tight monitor setups.
  • Durability: While flexible, micro-coax lines feature very fine conductors (often 36 to 44 AWG), making them more sensitive to excessive tensile pulling forces compared to the heavier copper cores found in traditional STP cables.

Cost and Manufacturing Complexity

The primary advantage of STP is cost. STP is a mature, automated manufacturing technology. Stripping, preparing, and soldering twisted pairs onto DisplayPort connector PCBs is standardized and inexpensive.

Micro-coaxial assemblies require specialized, automated laser stripping to expose the central conductor, dielectric, and shield without damage. Terminations often rely on dedicated micro-coax paddle boards or automated pulse-heat soldering. These factors make micro-coax DisplayPort cables noticeably more expensive to produce.

Summary

Shielded twisted pair remains the cost-effective standard for general-purpose, shorter DisplayPort 1.4 and entry-level DP 2.1 cables. However, for applications requiring high transmission rates (UHBR10, UHBR13.5, and UHBR20), thin profiles, tight bending, and low interference, micro-coaxial construction offers superior electrical and mechanical performance.
