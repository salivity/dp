How DisplayPort Prevents Audio Video Desync

Modern monitors and televisions frequently employ computationally intensive onboard video processing—such as motion smoothing, upscaling, local dimming calculations, and HDR tone mapping—which introduces noticeable display latency. To prevent audio-visual (AV) desynchronization over DisplayPort, systems rely on a combination of internal audio delay buffering, standardized latency reporting back to the source device, and tightly coupled clock regeneration. Together, these mechanisms ensure that audio either waits inside the display’s hardware buffer or is pre-delayed by the host source to perfectly match the time it takes to process and render a video frame.

Internal Hardware Audio Buffering

The most direct line of defense against lip-sync errors is the display's internal scalar and digital signal processing (DSP) architecture. When DisplayPort transmits audio, it encapsulates audio samples into Secondary Data Packets interleaved between active video lines on the main link.

When video processing introduces a delay (often between 10 to 100 milliseconds depending on the complexity of the algorithms enabled), the display does not play or output the audio immediately upon packet arrival. Instead, the monitor routes the incoming audio stream into an internal First-In, First-Out (FIFO) buffer. The display's firmware calculates the exact processing time required for the current video mode and holds the audio in the buffer, releasing it to the internal speakers or audio-out jack in exact lockstep with the corresponding scanned-out video frame.

Latency Reporting via EDID and DPCD

In scenarios where audio is routed upstream or handled by an external audio receiver rather than processed entirely inside the monitor, hardware buffering alone cannot prevent desync. In this case, DisplayPort relies on bidirectional metadata communication:

  • CTA-861 Latency Fields in EDID: The display provides its operational parameters to the host via Extended Display Identification Data (EDID). Within the CTA-861 extension blocks, the display can populate explicit "Video Latency" and "Audio Latency" fields. If a user activates a high-latency image processing mode (such as deep deinterlacing or dynamic noise reduction), the display reports this processing delay.
  • DisplayPort Configuration Data (DPCD): The DPCD registers, read via the AUX channel, allow dynamic handshake capabilities. When supported, the host graphics processing unit (GPU) or operating system reads the video processing delay and shifts the audio playback stream backward in time relative to the video, neutralizing the display's processing lag before the data ever leaves the computer.

Clock Regeneration and Drift Prevention

Latency mitigation prevents fixed offsets, but displays also require continuous synchronization to prevent temporal drift over long sessions. DisplayPort lacks a dedicated hardware clock wire; instead, it transmits audio and video across the main link using an embedded clock architecture.

To synchronize both media streams, DisplayPort uses fractional \(M\) and \(N\) divider values:

  • Video Time-Stamping: Uses \(M_{vid}\) and \(N_{vid}\) values embedded in the transport stream to reconstruct the pixel clock at the display.
  • Audio Clock Regeneration: Employs \(M_{aud}\) and \(N_{aud}\) values transmitted in the audio stream headers, deriving the original audio sample clock directly from the shared link symbol rate.

Because both the audio and video clocks are derived from the exact same physical link symbol clock, the display's timebase for audio cannot drift relative to its video clock. As long as the internal audio delay buffer correctly offsets the initial processing latency, the audio and video will remain continuously in phase throughout operation.
