DisplayPort HDCP Implementation Explained

This article provides an overview of how the DisplayPort interface integrates High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) to safeguard digital audio and video from unauthorized interception. It covers the core mechanics of the protection scheme, including device authentication over the Auxiliary Channel, high-speed data encryption across the main link lanes, the role of HDCP repeaters in Multi-Stream Transport (MST) topologies, and revocation mechanisms used to block compromised hardware.

DisplayPort implements HDCP primarily across two physical communication paths: the high-speed Main Link, which carries the encrypted multimedia payload, and the bidirectional Auxiliary (AUX) Channel, which manages the control, configuration, and cryptographic handshakes. Depending on the standard and bandwidth requirements, DisplayPort typically employs either HDCP 1.3 (adapted specifically for DisplayPort) or modern HDCP 2.2/2.3 specifications designed for 4K and higher-resolution protected streams.

The implementation process begins with Authentication and Key Exchange (AKE) over the AUX channel. When an HDCP-compliant source (such as a graphics card) connects to an HDCP-compliant sink (such as a monitor), the source initiates a handshake. The devices exchange public keys, random nonces, and digital certificates issued by Digital Content Protection LLC (DCP). The source verifies the sink’s certificate to confirm legitimacy. In modern HDCP 2.2+ implementations, an RSA- or ECDSA-based exchange generates a shared master key without exposing secrets to potential eavesdroppers. A locality check then ensures the connected display is physically adjacent, preventing transmission over long-distance networks.

Once authentication succeeds, the source and sink derive a shared session key used to encrypt the media stream. The actual video and audio data are encrypted before being multiplexed into DisplayPort’s micro-packet format. For HDCP 2.2 and above, the stream is encrypted using the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) with a 128-bit key in Counter (CTR) mode. This encrypted stream travels across the DisplayPort Main Link's high-speed lanes. Because the encryption occurs at the protocol layer, the physical transmission retains standard 8b/10b or 128b/132b line coding, ensuring signal integrity across the cable.

DisplayPort also accommodates complex Multi-Stream Transport (MST) environments, where a single physical port drives multiple daisy-chained displays or an MST hub. In these scenarios, intermediate nodes act as HDCP repeaters. An HDCP repeater authenticates with the upstream transmitter as a receiver, and simultaneously authenticates with downstream displays as a transmitter. The repeater builds a downstream topology tree and reports the number of connected devices, link depth, and downstream key lists back to the source. The source can allocate distinct virtual channels and cryptographic stream contexts, allowing it to send uniquely encrypted streams to individual downstream monitors over a single DisplayPort connection.

To ensure ongoing security, DisplayPort HDCP enforces continuous link synchronization and revocation. The sink regularly updates frame counters and status registers via the AUX channel. If the source detects a mismatch or loss of synchronization, the link drops the cipher and video output ceases. Additionally, media players and operating systems periodically push System Renewability Messages (SRMs) to the source device. These SRMs contain lists of revoked Public Key IDs belonging to compromised devices, preventing the source from completing an HDCP handshake with untrusted hardware.
