How LED Receiving Cards Decode DisplayPort Signals

This article provides a technical overview of how LED receiving cards process high-bandwidth DisplayPort (DP) video streams and convert them into discrete driving signals. LED receiving cards act as the bridge between standard high-speed video interfaces and modular LED panels, capturing gigabit-rate pixel data, decoding and mapping the video in an FPGA or ASIC, and translating those frames into the proprietary serial and PWM signals required by thousands of discrete constant-current LED driver ICs.

1. Physical Layer Reception and Link Training

The process begins at the physical layer (PHY) of the DisplayPort interface. DisplayPort transmits data across one, two, or four main differential lanes using AC-coupled high-speed serialized signals, alongside a bidirectional Auxiliary (AUX) channel.

When connected, the receiving card's onboard hardware—either a specialized DP receiver chip or an FPGA equipped with high-speed Multi-Gigabit Transceivers (MGTs)—executes link training over the AUX channel. This step negotiates the link rate (such as RBR, HBR, HBR2, or HBR3) and lane count, while setting appropriate voltage swing and pre-emphasis levels. Once trained, the receiving card’s Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) circuits lock onto the incoming serial stream, extract the embedded clock, and perform symbol alignment using 8b/10b (up to DP 1.4) or 128b/132b (DP 2.0+) decoding.

2. Stream Unpacking and Pixel Extraction

After line decoding, the raw data stream is organized into Micro-Packets containing Main Stream Attributes (MSA) and packed pixel data. The receiving card's core processing logic parses these packets to determine:

  • Horizontal and vertical active resolution
  • Refresh rates and frame blanking intervals
  • Color depth (such as 8-bit, 10-bit, or 12-bit per channel)
  • Color spaces (RGB or YCbCr 4:4:4 / 4:2:2)

The logic de-packetizes this stream, reconstructing a continuous raster image stored temporarily in high-speed frame buffers, typically external DDR3 or DDR4 SDRAM managed directly by the card's FPGA.

3. Spatial Mapping, Calibration, and Color Processing

Unlike consumer monitors that draw pixels linearly across a continuous panel, an LED display cabinet represents a specific coordinate window within a larger video wall. The receiving card extracts only the specific pixel region assigned to its cabinet.

Inside the FPGA, several pipeline operations occur simultaneously at high clock frequencies:

  • Pixel Mapping: The linear raster data is reordered to match the serpentine or distributed routing of the physical LED modules connected to the card.
  • Point-to-Point Calibration: Stored calibration coefficients (LUTs) adjust the brightness and chromaticity of individual LEDs to ensure uniform luminance across the display.
  • Gamma Correction and Grayscale Expansion: The linear video signal is converted to high-precision grayscale curves (often calculated at 16-bit to 22-bit internal precision) to maintain smooth low-light performance.

4. Protocol Translation for Discrete LED Drivers

Discrete LED driver ICs (such as those from Macroblock, Chipone, or SiliconCore) do not understand standard video protocols. Instead, they rely on synchronized serial protocols containing shift-register data, latch signals, and high-frequency grayscale clocks.

The receiving card synthesizes these control buses, which are routed through high-density connectors (or standard HUB75 headers) to the display modules:

  • Serial Data Lines (SDA/SIN): Serialized RGB data streams pushed into shift registers embedded within the driver ICs.
  • Shift Clock (DCLK): Clocks data into the driver IC shift registers at rates typically between 15 MHz and 35 MHz.
  • Latch Signals (LAT/LE): Pulses that instruct the driver ICs to transfer the shifted data into internal holding registers for display.
  • Scan Line Selection: Demultiplexer address lines (A, B, C, D, E) that activate specific rows of LEDs in time-multiplexed (multiplex/scan) panel architectures.
  • Grayscale Clock (GCLK): A continuous, high-speed pulse train generated by the FPGA (often reaching 20 MHz to 50 MHz or higher) that drives internal Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM) counters within the driver ICs.

5. Final Output Generation

Once data is latched into the driver ICs, the discrete drivers modulate constant-current sink outputs to illuminate the red, green, and blue LEDs according to the GCLK counter. Through this complete pipeline, multi-gigabit DisplayPort packet streams are continuously decoded, processed, and translated into parallel microsecond-level switching signals, producing stable, high-refresh-rate imagery on the LED panel.
