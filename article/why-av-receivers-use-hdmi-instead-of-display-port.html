Why AV Receivers Use HDMI Instead of DisplayPort

Audio/video (AV) receivers almost universally feature HDMI connections instead of DisplayPort because HDMI is the undisputed standard of the home theater ecosystem. While DisplayPort dominates desktop computer monitors, AV receivers are built to integrate living room electronics such as televisions, media streamers, gaming consoles, and Blu-ray players. The omission of DisplayPort comes down to ecosystem dominance, home-theater-specific features like ARC/eARC and CEC, physical cable distance limitations, and manufacturing economics.

The Consumer Electronics Ecosystem

The primary reason AV receivers omit DisplayPort is the fundamental division between consumer electronics and personal computing hardware. HDMI was founded by a consortium of consumer electronics giants—including Sony, Panasonic, and Toshiba—specifically to serve home entertainment systems. Conversely, DisplayPort was developed by the Video Electronics Standards Association (VESA) to succeed VGA and DVI in computer environments.

Because modern televisions, streaming devices (like Apple TV and Roku), cable boxes, and home consoles (PlayStation and Xbox) use HDMI exclusively, an AV receiver with DisplayPort would have virtually no compatible input or output devices in a typical living room.

Audio Return Channel (ARC/eARC) and CEC Support

AV receivers rely heavily on two core HDMI features that DisplayPort lacks in a consumer-ready capacity:

  • HDMI ARC/eARC (Audio Return Channel): This protocol allows a television to send uncompressed, high-bitrate surround sound (such as Dolby Atmos or DTS:X) back to the receiver through the same cable that sends video to the TV. DisplayPort does not offer an equivalent consumer standard for bi-directional television audio.
  • HDMI CEC (Consumer Electronics Control): CEC allows devices connected via HDMI to communicate commands to one another. It enables a single TV remote to power on the AV receiver, control receiver volume, and change inputs automatically. DisplayPort does not support CEC protocols, which would break the seamless plug-and-play experience users expect from modern home theaters.

Cable Length and Signal Integrity

Living room layouts typically require longer cable runs than desktop computer setups. Standard passive HDMI cables reliably carry 4K signals across 15 to 25 feet, with active options reaching much further. DisplayPort was engineered for short-distance desk connections, typically under 6 to 10 feet. At longer runs, passive DisplayPort signals degrade rapidly unless expensive active optical cables or signal repeaters are used, making it impractical for routing cables through walls or across large entertainment centers.

Cost and Bill of Materials

Including any port on an AV receiver requires dedicated hardware: physical chassis cutouts, controller chipsets, and board real estate. Adding DisplayPort inputs would increase the receiver's bill of materials (BOM) cost and engineering complexity. Because an infinitesimal fraction of home theater owners connect high-end PC towers directly to AV receivers via DisplayPort, manufacturers cannot justify the added manufacturing expense for a feature that would go unused by the vast majority of consumers.
