Why DisplayPort Monitors Cause DAC Audio Dropouts

Routing audio through a DisplayPort monitor to an external digital-to-analog converter (DAC) often leads to dropped samples, clicks, pops, or intermittent audio dropouts. This problem typically occurs because monitors are not engineered as dedicated audio transport devices, leading to severe clock jitter, packet prioritization conflicts over the DisplayPort stream, poor clock regeneration hardware in monitor scalers, and buffer underflows when display refresh rates fluctuate.

Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR) and Jitter

DisplayPort does not transmit audio as a continuous analog waveform or a dedicated raw I2S line. Instead, audio data is packetized and interleaved alongside high-bandwidth video data packets. Because there is no dedicated audio master clock line running through the cable, the receiving monitor must reconstruct the audio clock from the video stream using time stamps (Mvid and Nvid values) via an Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR) circuit.

Most external DACs, particularly high-resolution or audiophile-grade units, rely on precise Phase-Locked Loops (PLLs) to lock onto an incoming digital audio signal. The scaler chips inside consumer monitors use inexpensive, low-accuracy crystal oscillators and basic PLLs to regenerate this clock. This generates substantial timing jitter. When the reconstructed clock drifts beyond the tracking range or buffer tolerances of the external DAC, the DAC briefly loses synchronization, resulting in dropped samples and audible pops.

Bandwidth Interleaving and Variable Refresh Rates

Audio packets inside a DisplayPort pipeline are scheduled primarily during the horizontal and vertical blanking intervals of the video signal. If you operate at the limits of DisplayPort bandwidth—such as running high resolutions (4K or ultrawide) at high refresh rates (144Hz or higher)—the blanking intervals become extremely short.

This issue is amplified when using Variable Refresh Rate (VRR) technologies like G-Sync or FreeSync. As the monitor dynamically changes its refresh rate to match the frame output of your GPU, the timing and duration of the blanking intervals fluctuate continuously. The monitor’s internal audio buffer can easily run out of data (buffer underflow), causing the forwarded audio stream to stutter or drop packets entirely before reaching the DAC.

Inadequate Monitor Audio Buffering and Scaler Hardware

Display controllers prioritize video latency above all else. Consequently, the internal FIFO (First-In, First-Out) memory buffers dedicated to de-packetizing audio inside the monitor are minimal. If the monitor’s internal processing chip experiences a brief spike in workload—such as rendering an On-Screen Display (OSD) menu, changing color profiles, or managing power states—the audio pipeline is degraded first. The monitor fails to output a steady S/PDIF, optical, or analog stream to the downstream DAC, manifesting as audio cutouts.

Ground Loops and Electrical Noise

Many users route power or signal through the monitor via USB hubs or 3.5mm line-out jacks. Monitors contain noisy switch-mode power supplies, high-power backlights, and complex driving circuitry. This electrical noise can corrupt the signal integrity of coaxial S/PDIF connections or interfere with the sensitive internal reference clocks of USB DACs drawing power from the monitor, directly contributing to digital communication dropouts.

How to Resolve the Issue

  1. Connect Directly to the Host: Connect your DAC directly to your source PC using USB, PCIe, or motherboard optical/coaxial S/PDIF outputs to bypass the monitor’s audio processing entirely.
  2. Disable Variable Refresh Rate: If you must route audio through the monitor, turn off G-Sync or FreeSync to maintain consistent transmission intervals for audio packets.
  3. Adjust Display Settings: Lowering the display refresh rate (e.g., from 144Hz to 120Hz or 60Hz) can widen the video blanking interval, giving the monitor scaler more headroom to properly reconstruct the audio signal.
  4. Use Optical Isolation: If routing via S/PDIF from the monitor, an optical (TOSLINK) cable avoids electrical ground loop interference, though it will not fix hardware-level clock jitter inside the monitor.
