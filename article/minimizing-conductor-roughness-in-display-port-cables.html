Minimizing Conductor Roughness in DisplayPort Cables

High-performance DisplayPort standards, such as DisplayPort 2.1 with Ultra-High Bit Rate (UHBR) transmissions, operate at frequencies reaching tens of gigahertz where the "skin effect" forces electrical signals to travel along the outermost layer of a conductor. In this regime, microscopic surface roughness drastically increases insertion loss, signal attenuation, and phase jitter. This article outlines the primary advanced manufacturing techniques—including continuous casting, precision diamond die drawing, electropolishing, controlled atmospheric annealing, and high-purity electroplating—used by manufacturers to achieve mirror-like conductor surfaces and maintain signal integrity across premium DisplayPort cables.

Ohno Continuous Casting (OCC)

Standard electrolytic tough pitch (ETP) and oxygen-free copper (OFC) contain thousands of grain boundaries per meter. These boundaries act as micro-fissures and structural disruptions on the wire's surface.

To overcome this, high-end cable manufacturers utilize the Ohno Continuous Casting (OCC) process. By utilizing heated molds, the metal is cooled and solidified outside the mold, yielding single-crystal copper structures with virtually zero internal grain boundaries. The resulting conductor has an inherently smoother surface profile, eliminating the micro-notches and crystalline discontinuities that typically scatter high-frequency electromagnetic waves.

Precision Multi-Stage Diamond Die Drawing

Transforming cast metal into gauge-appropriate wire requires drawing the conductor through progressively smaller dies. Conventional steel or carbide dies wear unevenly, leaving longitudinal scratches, ridges, and burrs along the wire's perimeter.

Premium cable manufacturing employs laser-drilled, single-crystal diamond dies combined with advanced nano-lubrication. The extreme hardness and structural uniformity of single-crystal diamonds produce minimal friction during extrusion. Multiple intermediate drawing stages, with small reduction ratios per step, ensure that the metal is not torn or scored at a microscopic scale, significantly lowering the root-mean-square (RMS) surface roughness (\(R_q\)).

Controlled Atmosphere Vacuum Annealing

Cold-working copper through drawing dies introduces mechanical stress, work hardening, and surface micro-cracks. Annealing restores ductility and electrical conductivity by heating the wire to recrystallization temperatures.

If annealed in the presence of ambient oxygen, microscopic oxidation pits form across the wire's surface, creating severe surface roughness. Manufacturers prevent this by performing continuous inline annealing inside ultra-high vacuum (UHV) chambers or within inert, reducing atmospheres containing nitrogen and hydrogen. This preserves the surface profile achieved during drawing and prevents chemical degradation of the outermost skin layer.

Chemical and Electrochemical Polishing

Even under optimal drawing conditions, sub-micron peaks and valleys can remain. To achieve an ultra-smooth finish, specialized cables undergo chemical or electrochemical polishing (electropolishing).

In an electropolishing bath, the drawn wire acts as an anode in an acidic electrolyte. Electrical current selectively attacks and dissolves microscopic peaks faster than the surrounding valleys through differential current density. This leveling process rounds off jagged micro-asperities, reducing the total surface area and creating an isotropic, mirror-like finish ideal for gigahertz-level signal propagation.

High-Uniformity Silver Electroplating

Because silver possesses a higher electrical conductivity than copper, applying a thin silver layer over the copper core concentrates the signal in an even lower-loss medium.

To prevent this layer from worsening surface roughness, manufacturers use pulse electroplating or physical vapor deposition (PVD) techniques rather than standard direct-current (DC) plating. Pulse plating regulates nucleation rates at the atomic level, generating dense, fine-grained silver layers that conform to and fill remaining microscopic valleys on the copper surface. The resulting composite conductor features a smooth, low-resistance boundary that minimizes skin-effect losses at peak UHBR bandwidths.
