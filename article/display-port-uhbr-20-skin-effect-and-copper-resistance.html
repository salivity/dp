DisplayPort UHBR20 Skin Effect and Copper Resistance

This article examines how the skin effect impacts high-frequency electrical performance in DisplayPort Ultra-High Bit Rate 20 (UHBR20) copper cables. It explains the fundamental physics driving current to the conductor's outer boundary at 10 GHz frequencies, demonstrates how this physical constraint dramatically elevates effective AC resistance, details the exacerbating role of conductor surface roughness, and highlights the resulting engineering challenges for maintaining signal integrity over standard transmission lengths.

Understanding UHBR20 Frequencies

DisplayPort 2.1 introduces the UHBR20 transmission tier, which delivers 20 Gigabits per second (Gbps) per lane across four lanes, achieving a raw bandwidth of 80 Gbps. Because UHBR20 uses Non-Return-to-Zero (NRZ) signaling, a data rate of 20 Gbps corresponds to a fundamental frequency of 10 GHz (the Nyquist frequency). At these multi-gigahertz operational frequencies, the electrical characteristics of copper conductors diverge drastically from their standard direct current (DC) behavior due to electromagnetic phenomena.

The Mechanism of the Skin Effect

In a direct current or low-frequency alternating current (AC) circuit, electrons distribute uniformly across the entire cross-sectional area of a copper wire. As signal frequency escalates into the gigahertz realm, the alternating current generates an alternating internal magnetic field within the conductor. This magnetic field induces circulating eddy currents that oppose current flow in the center of the wire while reinforcing it near the perimeter.

As a result, current is pushed outward, concentrating in a thin outer layer known as the "skin depth" (\(\delta\)). The formula for skin depth is:

\[\delta = \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi \cdot f \cdot \mu}}\]

Where:

  • \(\rho\) is the electrical resistivity of copper (\(1.68 \times 10^{-8} \ \Omega\cdot\text{m}\))
  • \(f\) is the signal frequency (10 GHz for the UHBR20 fundamental)
  • \(\mu\) is the magnetic permeability of the material

At the 10 GHz fundamental frequency of UHBR20, the calculated skin depth in standard copper drops to approximately 0.66 micrometers (660 nanometers).

How the Skin Effect Alters Resistance

Resistance (\(R\)) is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area (\(A\)) through which current flows:

\[R = \frac{\rho \cdot L}{A}\]

When the effective cross-sectional area contracts from the full core of a typical conductor (such as 30 AWG or 32 AWG wire commonly used in display cables) to a hollow cylindrical ring only 0.66 microns deep:

  1. Dramatically Reduced Conductive Area: More than 98% of the wire's inner core becomes virtually inactive for signal transport. The effective conductive area is severely truncated.
  2. Square-Root Resistance Scaling: Conductor AC resistance increases proportionally to the square root of the frequency (\(\sqrt{f}\)). Compared to DC resistance, the effective resistance at 10 GHz increases by an order of magnitude.
  3. High-Frequency Harmonic Attenuation: High-speed digital signals consist not only of the 10 GHz fundamental but also higher harmonic frequencies required to form sharp square-wave edges. These higher harmonics experience even shallower skin depths and correspondingly higher resistance, causing dispersion and rounding the digital pulses.

The Compounding Effect of Surface Roughness

Because the skin depth at 10 GHz is sub-micron, the microscopic profile of the copper conductor plays a significant role in total resistance. Standard copper manufacturing leaves surface peaks and valleys (roughness) that often measure between 0.5 to 2.0 micrometers.

When the skin depth is equal to or smaller than the surface roughness profile:

  • Current must travel along the actual physical contours of the peaks and valleys rather than a straight path.
  • The effective path length of the current increases.
  • The actual cross-sectional layer is further distorted, inducing localized eddy current losses.

This interaction causes the effective AC resistance to rise well above standard theoretical skin-depth calculations, compounding signal attenuation.

Consequences for DisplayPort UHBR20 Systems

The sharp rise in AC resistance caused by the skin effect directly drives channel insertion loss (signal attenuation) in UHBR20 systems:

  • Restricted Cable Lengths: Passive copper cables operating at UHBR20 are typically limited to roughly 0.8 to 1.0 meters. Beyond this distance, resistive signal loss degrades the signal below the receiver's minimum detection threshold.
  • Need for Active Equalization: DisplayPort UHBR20 receivers rely on advanced Continuous Time Linear Equalization (CTLE) and Decision Feedback Equalization (DFE) within the PHY layer to reconstruct signals degraded by frequency-dependent resistive losses.
  • Shift to Alternative Interconnects: Longer UHBR20 connections increasingly require Active Copper Cables (ACC) containing redrivers or retimers to re-amplify the signal mid-span, or Active Optical Cables (AOC) to bypass metallic high-frequency resistance entirely.
