DisplayPort Active Repeater Latency Explained

DisplayPort active signal repeaters are widely used to extend cable reach and maintain high-bandwidth signal integrity for high-resolution, high-refresh-rate displays. This article explores the latency characteristics introduced by these devices, breaking down the operational differences between redrivers and retimers, measuring the exact magnitude of the added delay, and evaluating its real-world performance impact on gaming, VR, and professional workflows.

Types of Repeaters: Redrivers vs. Retimers

The latency added by a DisplayPort repeater depends entirely on the architecture of the signal restoration integrated circuit (IC):

  • Redrivers (Analog Equalizers): Redrivers use purely analog conditioning circuits to counteract high-frequency attenuation caused by long copper traces or cables. They amplify and equalize the incoming electrical signal without decoding the bits or re-clocking the data. Because there is no digital conversion or buffering, redrivers introduce negligible latency, typically between 100 picoseconds and 2 nanoseconds.
  • Retimers (Digital Clock and Data Recovery): Retimers contain full Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) circuits. They sample the incoming signal, extract the clock, de-jitter the signal, and reconstitute the digital bitstream before re-transmitting it. This process requires internal state machines and small FIFO (First-In, First-Out) memory buffers to manage phase differences. As a result, retimers introduce higher latency than redrivers, typically ranging between 5 nanoseconds and 50 nanoseconds.

Physical Layer Operation vs. Frame Buffering

A common misconception is that active repeaters act like video scalers, video processors, or capture cards, which can introduce several milliseconds of input lag due to frame buffering.

Active DisplayPort repeaters operate strictly at the Physical Layer (PHY) of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model. They do not interpret DisplayPort Main Link protocol packets, nor do they buffer lines or frames of video data. The signal passes through the device as a continuous serial stream. Consequently, active repeaters introduce zero frame-level delay.

Latency in Context: Propagation Delay vs. Processing Delay

To understand the delay introduced by a repeater, it must be evaluated alongside the inherent physical latency of the cable itself:

  • Electrical Propagation Delay: Electrical signals travel through copper cables at approximately 60% to 70% of the speed of light, yielding a propagation delay of roughly 5 nanoseconds per meter.
  • A Typical Active Setup: A high-quality 10-meter active DisplayPort cable with an integrated retimer will introduce approximately 50 nanoseconds of cable propagation delay plus 10 to 30 nanoseconds of retimer processing delay, totaling around 60 to 80 nanoseconds.

Real-World Performance Impact

One millisecond equals 1,000,000 nanoseconds. A single frame at a 144 Hz refresh rate lasts approximately 6.94 milliseconds, and at 240 Hz, a frame lasts 4.16 milliseconds.

Because the maximum processing delay added by even the most complex DisplayPort retimer is well below 100 nanoseconds (0.0001 milliseconds), the latency introduced is completely undetectable by human senses. Active repeaters do not degrade input responsiveness in competitive gaming, disrupt frame timing in virtual reality (VR) headsets, or cause audio-video desynchronization in professional production environments.
