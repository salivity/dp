DisplayPort 1.4: Eliminating Dual Cables for 4K & 5K

Early ultra-high-resolution monitors, including original 5K displays and high-refresh-rate 4K panels, initially required two physical DisplayPort cables running in parallel to bypass the bandwidth bottlenecks of the era. The release of the DisplayPort 1.4 standard fundamentally changed this requirement by introducing High Bit Rate 3 (HBR3) transmission modes and Display Stream Compression (DSC 1.2). Together, these features expanded available data throughput and compressed pixel data without visual degradation, successfully consolidating video, audio, and sync signals onto a single cable.

The Bandwidth Bottleneck of DisplayPort 1.2

Before DisplayPort 1.4, high-end monitors relied on DisplayPort 1.2, which used High Bit Rate 2 (HBR2) signaling. This standard capped effective data throughput at 17.28 Gbps. While sufficient for standard 4K displays operating at 60Hz with 8-bit color, it fell significantly short of the data demands posed by higher specifications:

  • 5K at 60Hz: A 5120x2880 resolution at 60Hz with 24-bit RGB color requires approximately 22.12 Gbps of uncompressed data bandwidth.
  • 4K at High Refresh Rates: A 3840x2160 resolution at 120Hz or 144Hz pushes uncompressed bandwidth well beyond 25 Gbps, rising even higher when 10-bit color (HDR) is enabled.

Because a single DisplayPort 1.2 connection could not carry these signals, manufacturers were forced to treat a single display as two distinct virtual panels side-by-side using Multi-Stream Transport (MST). This required users to plug two separate DisplayPort cables from their graphics card into the monitor. This workaround frequently caused operational issues, such as screen tearing down the center seam, mismatched scaling, broken full-screen mode in games, and an inability to use Variable Refresh Rate (VRR) technologies like AMD FreeSync or NVIDIA G-Sync.

The Upgrade to High Bit Rate 3 (HBR3)

DisplayPort 1.4 addressed physical transmission limits by introducing HBR3. Operating across the same physical 4-lane architecture, HBR3 raised the raw transmission rate from 5.4 Gbps per lane to 8.1 Gbps per lane.

This upgraded the total bandwidth from 21.6 Gbps to 32.4 Gbps, resulting in an effective usable data payload of 25.92 Gbps after encoding overhead. This raw speed bump was large enough to drive a 5K display at 60Hz using standard 8-bit color depth natively over a single wire, eliminating the fundamental reason dual cables were required for baseline productivity resolutions.

Display Stream Compression (DSC 1.2)

While HBR3 solved basic single-cable 5K transmission, driving 4K at 144Hz with 10-bit HDR or pushing 5K displays at higher refresh rates still exceeded the 25.92 Gbps ceiling. To permanently eliminate the dual-cable requirement across all display modes, DisplayPort 1.4 integrated Display Stream Compression version 1.2 (DSC).

DSC is a VESA-standardized, visually lossless, low-latency compression algorithm that operates in real time at the hardware level. Key characteristics include:

  • Up to 3:1 Compression Ratio: DSC can compress the required data rate by up to three times without introducing human-detectable artifacts.
  • No Latency Penalty: Compression and decompression happen in line within microseconds, preventing input lag for gaming and critical workflows.
  • Feature Preservation: Unlike older subsampling methods (such as 4:2:0 or 4:2:2 chroma subsampling, which cause blurry text), DSC preserves full 4:4:4 color depth and native HDR dynamic range.

The Outcome

By coupling HBR3 link speeds with DSC 1.2, DisplayPort 1.4 provided the effective pipeline necessary to push up to 8K at 60Hz or 4K at high refresh rates with full HDR over a single physical connection. This eliminated the split-panel MST architecture, restored native Variable Refresh Rate capabilities, simplified cable management, and ended the era of dual-cable display setups.
